Every toolpath type, setting and check in 454 Design’s CAM, by topic. For a walkthrough from drawing to G-code, see the [Design quick start](design-quickstart.html).

**CAM is in beta.** Toolpaths are new and still being tested on real projects, which is still finding problems. Check every job before cutting material: open it in 454 Control and watch the preview all the way through, read the Checks tab, then run it in the air with Z zeroed well above the material. Every G-code file 454 Design saves starts with a note saying so. If something looks wrong, <a href="https://github.com/ctredway/454-workshop/issues" target="_blank" rel="noopener">report it on GitHub<span class="sr-only"> (opens in a new tab)</span></a>, with the drawing if you can.

## The basics

**The Toolpaths panel** sits on the right. Select shapes on the drawing, then press **New toolpath** (the + icon). The editor takes the panel's place while it's open, a little wider, so the drawing and its live preview stay in view. Its settings sit under headings (Tool, Depth, Passes, Entry and exit, Tabs), and only the ones that apply to the kind of cut are shown. Click or tab into any setting and the box above the buttons says what it does, with a small picture for stepover, ramp, lead in and tabs. Press **Create** to add the toolpath, or **Cancel**, and the list of toolpaths comes back. Each toolpath gets a card in the panel, where you can edit it, move it up or down, show or hide its preview, leave it out of the G-code, recalculate it, or delete it. Right-click a card for the same actions in a menu.

**The row of icons** under the panel's name holds what you do with the whole job: **Preview in wood**, **Preview in 454 Control** and **Job sheet**, then **Save template** and **Apply template**. Rest the pointer on one to see its name; the ⓘ at the end of the row explains them. **Save G-code…** is the wide button under the list.

**Naming a toolpath.** Type a name in the editor's **Name** box, or double-click the name on a card (or right-click it and choose **Rename…**), type, and press Enter. The name is shown on the card and the job sheet and written into the G-code, so 454 Control shows it too. Leave it empty and the toolpath is named after its kind of cut, such as "Outside profile"; the kind of cut is always shown on the line under the name.

**Folding cards.** The small arrow at the left of a card folds it to its title, so a long list fits on the screen; the arrow opens it again. The button with three lines in the panel's header folds or opens every card at once. A folded card shows ⚠ beside its name when the toolpath has a warning, such as cutting past the material: hold the pointer over it to read why.

**Recalculating** rebuilds a toolpath from its shapes and the material as they are now, keeping its own settings (tool, depths, feeds). The **Recalculate** button (↻) on each card is highlighted when a toolpath is out of date, because its shapes or the material changed since it was made; **Recalculate all** in the panel’s header does every toolpath at once. A toolpath keeps its own copy of the tool’s feeds and speeds, so after changing a tool in the library, edit the toolpath to pick up the new values.

**Nothing is guessed.** A new toolpath starts with no tool and no depth, and **Create** stays disabled until you’ve chosen both. G-code can’t be saved while any toolpath has no tool. Z zero, the material’s thickness and where X0 Y0 sits all come from **Job setup**.

**The hint** under the editor’s settings says what the toolpath will do in plain words (depth, passes, rings, tabs, finishing), and starts with ⚠ when something is wrong. Read it before pressing **Create**.

**Cutting past the material is always said.** Every toolpath is checked against the material's thickness
(Job setup), from the deepest point it really cuts, whatever kind it is and however its depth was set. One
that goes past the bottom says so with ⚠ in the editor's hint and on its card: "goes 15.00 mm deep: through the
12.00 mm material and 3.00 mm into the spoilboard". **Save G-code** and **Preview in 454 Control** stop and
list them first, with **Cancel** or **Save anyway** (cutting into the spoilboard is sometimes meant). A change
that puts a toolpath through, such as a thinner material or a [parameter](design-tools#parameters), names it
straight away. A through cut's own overcut is meant, so it isn't flagged. If the material's thickness isn't
set, depths can't be checked, and Save G-code says so.

**Depth and tabs from parameters.** The **Depth**, tab **Length** and tab **Thickness** boxes take a
parameter, or arithmetic with one: `material / 2`, `dado_depth`, `tab_thk`. The toolpath remembers it (its
card shows **9.00 mm = material / 2**) and is recalculated when the parameter or the material changes. A
depth from a parameter needs the material's thickness set, so it can always be checked. If a toolpath's
parameter can't be worked out (it was deleted, or the thickness was cleared), its card says so and **Save
G-code refuses** until it's put right. Arithmetic without names (`6 + 2`) is just worked out, and a plain
number unlinks the box.

**Any closed shape can be cut**: rectangles, circles, closed outlines, **groups** (such as a traced logo) and **text**. A group’s outlines, or a word’s letters, count as one shape.

## Profile

Cuts along an outline. Choose **Profile, outside** (the part keeps the outline’s size), **Profile, inside** (a hole keeps its size), or **Profile, on the line** (the cutter’s centre follows the line).

<Shot src={camProfile} alt="An outside profile around a rounded plate: the toolpath runs just outside the outline, with four tabs marked on it." caption="Profile, outside, cutting through, with four tabs. It's selected here, which is when its tabs are drawn." />

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Cut depth** | How deep, from Z zero. Or tick **through** to cut the material’s full thickness. |
| **Overcut** | With **through**: how far past the bottom of the material to cut, so parts come free cleanly. |
| **Per pass** | The depth of each pass. Filled from the tool’s feeds and speeds for the chosen material. |
| **Feed** | Cutting speed. Also from the library; plunge rate and spindle speed come with it. |

Profiles also take [finishing, direction, leads, tabs and ramps](#finishing-direction-leads-tabs-ramps).

## Pocket

**Pocket (clear the area)** removes everything inside an outline to a depth. **Islands are automatic**: shapes inside the pocket’s outline (a letter’s hole, a boss) are left standing.

<Shot src={camPocketOffset} alt="A pocket with a round island, cleared with offset rings that follow the outline inwards and flow around the island." caption="Offset rings: each pass follows the outline's shape, working outwards from the middle to the walls; the island is left standing." />

<Shot src={camPocketRaster} alt="The same pocket cleared with raster: straight lines back and forth across it, then a pass round the walls and the island." caption="Raster: straight passes back and forth, then one pass round the walls and the island." />

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Cut depth**, **Per pass** | As for profiles. |
| **Stepover** | How far apart the clearing passes are, as a share of the cutter’s diameter. 40% is the default. |
| **Clearing** | **Offset rings** work outwards from the middle, following the pocket’s shape. **Raster** runs back and forth in straight lines **at** the angle you set (0° left to right, 90° front to back), then once round the walls. Raster is often quicker on long or rectangular pockets. |

Pockets ramp into each depth by default (see [Ramps](#ramps)), and link passes with short feed moves only where the cutter stays inside the pocket; otherwise it lifts, moves and ramps back in. Pockets also take [finishing and direction](#finishing-direction-leads-tabs-ramps).

**If the cutter is too big** for part of a pocket, that part is left and the hint says so. A narrow strip the cutter can’t reach needs a smaller tool.

### Clearing with a larger bit, then a smaller one

A large bit clears a big pocket quickly but leaves its corners round and can't get into narrow parts. A small
bit reaches them but takes a long time over the whole pocket. Use both:

1. Make the pocket with the **large bit**, as usual.
2. Select the same shapes and make a second pocket with the **small bit**. Under **Clean up after**, choose the
   large bit's pocket.

The small bit then cuts only where the large one couldn't reach: the corners it left round, and any part too
narrow for it. Typically that's a quarter of the cutting a full pocket with the small bit would take, or
less. The clean-up goes as deep as the large bit's pocket, runs after it, and follows it if you change it.

**Clean up after** lists pockets on the same shapes with a larger bit. If that pocket is deleted, changed to
other shapes, or unticked, the clean-up says so; if it's deleted, the clean-up clears the whole pocket, so
nothing is left uncut.

## Drill

**Drill at centres** drills at the centre of each selected circle, nearest hole next.

<Shot src={camDrill} alt="Eight holes in two rows of four inside a plate, each marked with a ring and cross, joined by dashed rapid moves in drilling order." caption="Drilling: a ring at each hole, and the dashed rapids show the order, nearest hole next." />

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Cut depth** or **through** | How deep each hole goes. |
| **Peck** | How far to drill before lifting to clear chips. **0** drills each hole in one plunge. |

## Chamfer

**Chamfer edges (V-bit)** bevels an edge with a V-bit.

<Shot src={camChamfer} alt="A rounded rectangle with its chamfer toolpath highlighted along the edge." caption="A chamfer is one pass along the edge: the V-bit's depth sets the bevel's width." />

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Bevel width** | How wide the bevel is on the top face. The depth follows from the bit’s angle. |
| **Bit** | The V-bit’s included angle, filled from the library. |
| **The line is** | **the part’s edge (tip on it)**: the bevel runs down to the line. **the bevel’s top, going inside** or **going outside**: the line is where the bevel starts, as VCarve’s “vectors at top”. Going inside countersinks a hole to its drawn size. |

A bevel wider than the bit can cut is refused, with the reason in the hint.

## V-carve

**V-carve (V-bit)** carves text, logos and artwork with a V-bit. The bit’s tip follows each shape’s centre-line, deeper where the shape is wide and rising to the surface at sharp points, so the carving’s edges land exactly on the outline and corners come out sharp. There’s no depth to set: the shape decides it.

<Shot src={camVcarve} alt="The number 454 V-carved: the toolpath follows each stroke's centre-line, fanning out into the corners, with extra clearing passes in the wide part of the 5." caption="V-carving “454”: the tip follows each stroke's centre-line, reaching out into the corners." />

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Bit** | The V-bit’s included angle, from the library. |
| **Max depth** | Optional. Deeper than this, the carving gets a flat floor. A **clearing pocket** is added with it for the flat middles, runs first, and follows if you change the max depth; open it and choose its end mill. |
| **Tip** | The width of the bit’s flat tip, if it has one (engraving bits usually do). From the library. Lines narrower than the tip stay at the surface. |

**Warnings** in the hint: the tool isn’t a V-bit; the carving is wider than the bit can make (set a max depth, or use a larger bit); or it would go deeper than the material.

## Inlay

**Inlay (V-bit pocket or plug)** makes the two halves of a V-carve inlay from the same shapes, so they fit. Cut both with the **same V-bit**.

<Shot src={camInlay} alt="On the left, a star with its inlay pocket cleared in rings. On the right, a mirrored star inside a square boundary, with the plug's V-carve along its walls and clearing between the star and the boundary." caption="The pocket in the base (left), and the plug (right), which Design makes on a mirrored copy with its boundary, on an “Inlay plug” layer beside the design." />

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Cut the** | **pocket (in the base)** or **plug (in the inlay piece)**. |
| **Pocket depth** | How deep the pocket’s flat floor is. 5 mm to start. |
| **Start depth** | Plug only: how deep the plug’s walls begin. The plug seats this deep in the pocket. 2.5 mm to start. |
| **Plug depth** | Plug only: how deep the plug is carved. More than the start depth, so it stands proud. 5 mm to start. |

**Create** makes each half as two ordinary toolpaths: a clearing pocket (choose its end mill) and a V-carve. The **plug** half also draws a mirrored copy of your shapes, and a boundary around them, on an **Inlay plug** layer beside the design.

**How it fits:** flipped into the pocket, the plug wedges on its walls at the start depth, leaves *pocket depth − start depth* underneath for glue, and stands *plug depth − start depth* above the surface to plane off. The hint states all three.

**Trim the inlay piece to the boundary before gluing.** Stock left outside it hits the base and stops the plug seating.

## Finishing, direction, leads, tabs, ramps

These settings appear in the editor for the toolpath types they apply to. Here, a profile's editor with
tabs and arc leads switched on:

<Shot src={camProfileEditor} alt="The toolpath editor for an outside profile: cutting through, a 6.35 mm cutter, depth per pass, feed, direction, finishing, arc leads, and four tabs with their length and thickness." />

### Finishing

Profiles (inside and outside) and pockets: **Finishing** — **leave** an amount on the walls during the roughing passes, then, with **then finish it** ticked, take it off in one pass at full depth. The cutter is barely loaded on that pass, so walls come out cleaner and truer. Untick it to leave the material for another toolpath or tool.

### Direction

Profiles, pockets and chamfers: **Climb** (the default) usually leaves the better finish on a rigid machine; **Conventional** can suit flexible setups or brittle material.

### Lead in/out

Inside and outside profiles: **Arc** (a quarter circle that meets the line tangentially) or **Line** (at 45°), sized by the box beside it (the cutter’s diameter by default). The cutter sweeps onto the line from the waste side and off it again, so no pass starts or stops on the finished wall, and the path starts mid-way along the longest straight edge.

- **Passes that ramp in** get only the lead-out: the ramp is already a gentle way in. Passes that plunge (**Ramp in** set to
  **Plunge**), and a **finishing pass**, which drops straight into the roughed slot, get both.
- **In a tight hole** the lead shrinks to fit, and is left out if even a small one would touch the wall.

### Start point

Profiles (outside, inside and on the line): **Start** says where each shape's cut begins. **Automatic**
starts at a corner of the path, or with leads in the middle of the longest edge. To choose, press **Set
start** and click the outline where you want it: on an edge that's cleaned up anyway, at the grain end, or away
from a detail, so the small mark where each pass begins lands somewhere harmless. One start per shape: click
again to move it. Press **Done** when finished, or **Automatic** to go back. The start shows on the drawing as a
ring with a dot. Lead-ins, ramps and tabs work from the chosen start, and a start on a tab never cuts into it.

<Shot src={camStartPoint} alt="An outside profile around a rounded plate, with a ring and a dot on the outline's top edge, towards the right." caption="A chosen start: the ring with a dot, on the top edge. Each pass of this profile begins there." />

### Tabs

Profiles: tick **Add tabs**, then either click **Edit tabs** and click the outline to add a tab (click a tab to remove it), or type how many and press **Add** to space them evenly round each shape. **Clear** removes them all. Set their **Length** and **Thickness**. Both are the wood a tab leaves holding the part: the length along the middle of the cut, whatever the size of the bit (the bit stays up for the tab's length and its own width, so its round ends don't eat into the tab), and the thickness measured up from the bottom of the material, so a cut through with an overcut still leaves the full thickness. Choose their **Shape**:

- **Flat** (the default): the cutter steps straight up at one end of the tab, runs level across it, and steps straight down at the other end, leaving a small flat-topped bridge.
- **3D (tapered)**: the cutter ramps up from the cut's floor to the tab's full thickness at its middle, and back down to the floor, leaving a triangular bridge as long as the tab's length. 3D tabs are easier to cut through when freeing the part, and leave a smaller mark to clean up.

Either way, only the passes below a tab's top go over it, and tabs are kept on finishing passes too.

A new toolpath starts with the tabs you used last: their number, length, thickness and shape. The first time,
that's 4 tabs, 4 mm long and 1.5 mm thick, flat. Thin tabs can let a part break free while the cutter is still
going round it, so check the thickness suits the wood before you cut. **Preview in wood** shows tabs in amber.

### Ramps

Profiles and pockets: **Ramp in** sets how each pass goes down into the material.

- **Ramp** (the default): the cutter slopes down along the cut, at the plunge rate, over the length in the box
  beside it. Leave the box empty for four cutter diameters (at least 4 mm), which follows the cutter if you
  change it; type a length to set your own. On a profile the passes run into each other without stopping:
  each lap ends back at the start and the cutter carries straight on, ramping down to the next depth as it
  goes. After the last pass it carries on past the start and cuts the ramped stretch again at full depth, so
  a through-cut never leaves a sloping web holding the part. Pockets ramp along their rings, or back and forth
  along a line when rastering. Converted VCarve toolpaths keep VCarve’s ramp length.
- **Plunge**: the cutter goes straight down at the plunge rate, then cuts. Use it with a cutter that can
  plunge (a centre-cutting end mill), and a plunge rate to suit.

Ramping is easier on the cutter and the machine than plunging, especially in hardwood and with small
cutters. On a profile, a ramp is different from a [lead](#lead-inout): a ramp comes down along the line; a
lead comes onto the line from the side, at depth. The toolpath's card and the job sheet show which it does
(**Entry**), and the hint under the settings says how far it ramps.

## Tools and materials

Choosing a tool opens the **tool library**. Pick the machine and material in its bar; the toolpath takes that tool’s feeds and speeds for that material. **Recently used** at the top holds your last six.

- **Edit** a tool: name, type, units, diameter, angle and flat tip for V-bits, flutes, notes, and its tool number on the chosen machine.
- **Feeds and speeds are per material**: with a material chosen, **Edit** shows that tool’s feed, plunge, spindle speed, pass depth and stepover for it. A material with none yet offers **Add feeds**, started from another material’s (a note says which, and in which units).
- **+ Tool**, **Duplicate**, **Delete**, **+ Material**, and **Import VCarve library…** (reads VCarve’s `tools.vtdb`). No VCarve? **Start an empty library**.

**Toolpaths keep their own feeds.** Editing the library doesn’t change toolpaths already made, so a job you’ve checked can’t change under you. Choose the tool again in a toolpath to take the new values. The library lives in this browser and is shared with 454 Control.

## Order, checking and saving

### Order

A new toolpath slots into the usual cutting order: **holes, chamfers, pockets, V-carves, inside profiles, on-the-line profiles, outside profiles**, so the part is held until the end. Reorder with the arrows on each card; an order you’ve arranged is kept, and editing never moves a toolpath. A toolpath that would run after the outside profile has cut the part free gets a warning.

### Check

**Check** looks over the drawing before cutting: open shapes, shapes in pieces, doubled shapes, crossings, and shapes no toolpath cuts. It lists what needs a look, selects those shapes on the drawing, and offers to fix the ones it can.

### Preview in wood

**Preview in wood**, the first icon in the row at the top of the Toolpaths panel, shows the material as it will look after the ticked toolpaths,
as a block of wood in 3D: pockets with their floors and rounded corners, V-carving with its slopes, tabs
holding the parts, and through-cuts showing the spoilboard underneath.

**Drag** to turn it and look from any side, **right-drag** (or Shift+drag) to move it, and **scroll** to zoom.
**Angled**, **Top** and **Front** put the view back square. On a computer that can't show 3D, it shows the
same thing from above, shaded.

It's worked out from the toolpaths' own moves, in order, each cut by its bit's real shape: a flat end mill, a
ball-nose (from the tool library), or a V-bit at its angle and tip, on a grid about 0.1 mm fine for a small
job. So a wrong depth, a pocket that leaves an island, or a part with no tabs shows here before any wood is
cut. It also says the deepest cut, and warns in red if a rapid move would cut wood.

Use it as well as, not instead of, the preview and air run in 454 Control, which check the G-code itself
with the machine's own limits.

### Saving G-code

**Save G-code…** writes every included toolpath to one file, in order, with a tool change wherever the tool changes, numbered from your library, and each toolpath’s name so 454 Control can show which one is running. It refuses while any toolpath has no tool chosen.

**One file per bit:** when a job uses more than one bit, Save G-code asks whether to save **One file** (454 Control
stops at each bit change and says which bit to fit) or **One file per bit**. Per bit, you pick a folder and it
saves a file for each stretch of the job cut with one bit, in cutting order, named so they sort in order:
`Sign - 1 of 3 - T1 6.35 mm cutter.nc`, `Sign - 2 of 3 - T2 60° V-bit.nc`, and so on. A bit used again later
gets another file rather than moving its cuts earlier, so nothing is cut out of order (a profile that frees a
part never comes before the carving on it). Each file says at the top which file it is. Run them in order,
and keep Z zero and XY zero the same between them. It asks before replacing files already in the folder.

**Templates:** for jobs you make again and again (signs, boxes, cutting boards), **Save template** (the fourth icon in the row at the
top of the Toolpaths panel) saves the toolpaths' settings to a file: bits, depths, passes, feeds, tabs, ramps,
finishing and their order, but not the shapes. **Apply template** (the fifth icon) makes them again in another drawing. Each
toolpath finds its shapes by the **name of the layer** they were on, so draw the parts of the job on layers
named for what happens to them (say "Carve" and "Cut out"), and use the same names next time.

Before making anything, it lists each toolpath with the shapes it found, and any it can't make (no layer of
that name, or nothing on it). They're added after any toolpaths already there, with tabs spaced evenly, and
Undo takes them all back. Depths that cut through use the new drawing's material thickness, so set that
first, and check each toolpath before cutting.

**Preview in 454 Control**, the second icon in that row, loads exactly the G-code Save G-code would write into 454 Control, for its 3D
preview and playback, its time estimate (with your machine's acceleration) and its checks, with no file to
save or open. Nothing is sent to the machine. If Control is already open, the job goes to it, without
reloading it or disconnecting the machine; in the desktop app its window comes forward, and on the website
the job is waiting in your Control tab. If Control isn't open, it opens with the job. While a job is running
on the machine, Control keeps its loaded file and says so.

<Compare kind="same" title="Same as Carbide Motion">

The G-code is plain GRBL: tool changes are M6 with a T number, which 454 Control and Carbide Motion both handle with their tool-change routine.

</Compare>

### Job sheet

**Job sheet**, the third icon in that row, puts everything about the job on one
printable page, to keep at the machine: the material and its thickness, a picture of the job to scale
with each toolpath numbered where it starts, a setup checklist (where to zero X, Y and Z, the first
tool, how far the cutting reaches so clamps stay clear), the tools, every toolpath in cutting order with
its tool, feeds, depths and estimated time, and anything that needs attention. Click the job name or
**Notes** to edit them before printing; **Print…** prints it, or saves it as a PDF.

<Shot src={jobSheet} alt="The top of a job sheet: the 454 logo, the job name Workshop sign, the material size, thickness, toolpath and tool counts, estimated time, and the job drawn to scale with four numbered toolpaths." caption="The top of a job sheet. The tools, the toolpaths in cutting order, and notes follow." />

The tool numbers (T1, T2...) are the ones in the G-code, and the sheet describes exactly what **Save
G-code** will write: toolpaths left out of the G-code are left out of the sheet too, and any that are out
of date are recalculated first. In a multi-sheet project, it covers the chosen sheet.

:::note
The times are estimates at the programmed feeds, without acceleration, so real jobs run somewhat longer.
454 Control's estimate, which uses your machine's own settings, is closer.
:::

## VCarve projects

Opening a VCarve `.crv` brings in its drawing, material and zero, and its toolpaths as previews. **Make these editable** converts them into 454 toolpaths, keeping VCarve’s tools, depths, passes, feeds, order and settings:

- **Profiles**, with tabs, ramps, allowance and direction
- **Pockets**, including raster clearing and its angle
- **Drilling**, and **chamfers** in both of VCarve’s modes
- **V-carves**, with the bit’s angle and tip and the flat depth

**How shapes are found:** each toolpath in a VCarve project records which vectors it uses, by VCarve’s own IDs, and 454 reads that record. It doesn’t depend on where anything is, so it works even if parts were moved after VCarve last calculated. For a project without that record, 454 falls back to matching VCarve’s saved toolpath previews to the shapes; if nothing matches, the toolpath comes across with no shapes: open it with **Edit** and pick them. Inlay toolpaths aren’t converted yet.

**Multi-sheet projects:** each sheet comes in as a sheet, named as in VCarve, with one shown at a time. Choose the sheet at the top of the Toolpaths panel: it shows that sheet, lists its toolpaths, and **Save G-code** saves that sheet only, named for it. Each sheet is its own job, cut from its own material, so a toolpath can’t mix shapes from different sheets. See [Sheets](design-workspace.html#sheets) for adding your own.

## How the toolpaths are checked

Every toolpath type is tested the same way before release: every point along every move is checked to keep the full cutter radius from every wall and island, and every reachable point is checked to be cut. V-carves and inlays are checked against exact answers (a V-carved 10 mm bar comes out 8.662 mm deep against an exact 8.660), and an inlay’s fit is proved by simulating both halves and pushing the plug in. Converted toolpaths are compared with VCarve’s own paths.
